01 · Capstone, Laboratory of Analysis and Modeling of Processes · Politecnico di Milano

Redesigning a €50M delivery provider, and proving it pays back in Year 2

At a furniture delivery-and-assembly company, rescheduled deliveries were eroding service and slowing revenue growth. We rebuilt its processes, organization and information system, then tested the redesign on its own data before asking for a single euro.

Team of 5Process redesignMonte CarloNPVSAP Signavio

The problem

Logiflex delivers and assembles furniture through several local hubs. Crews were sized only quarter by quarter and often did not match the weekly delivery plan, deliveries were rescheduled for transport, assembly and customer-side reasons, and nobody owned the process end to end. The case is didactic: inspired by a real company, with course-provided operating data.

My role

I led the redesign of the processes: introducing new value-added steps, retiring obsolete ones, and restructuring the organization around process owners. I designed the integrated cloud system for internal file management, mapped the TO-BE processes in SAP Signavio, and worked on the financial feasibility (Monte Carlo and NPV), the KPI dashboard and the technology risk assessment.

What we did

  • Cleaned 1,483 crew-month records down to 1,390 and fitted empirical distributions for each rescheduling cause.
  • Ran 1,000 Monte Carlo simulations of the AS-IS and TO-BE processes to measure service effectiveness (customers served ÷ delivery requests).
  • Redesigned planning: quarterly crew, fleet and equipment sizing per hub, re-forecast monthly, tracked by a forecast-accuracy KPI.
  • Built a 7-KPI dashboard with owners and targets, and scored the redesign’s impact at 3.76 out of 5.
  • Sized the investment at €457K within a €500K budget and modeled NPV at a 10% cost of capital, with sensitivity analysis.

02 · Quality Data Analysis · Politecnico di Milano

Catching 14 of 15 defects without a single false alarm

The brief came from an off-Earth manufacturing scenario: robotic 3D printing of composite structures in orbit or on the Moon, where no technician can inspect the parts. We had to turn camera images into metrics and control charts that flag defects on their own. The core difficulty is one every risk team knows: more sensors mean more false alarms, unless you control the error rate across all of them.

Team of 4Top scorePythonOpenCVSVD / PCAHotelling T²

The problem

We calibrated on a training set of 132 images of in-control printing, then faced a blind test set of 23 images containing 15 real structural defects. The first design, with each chart tuned on its own, caught only 3 of the 15.

My role

The team developed two methodologies: a simpler one based on the course material, and an advanced one drawn from the academic papers we were given. I proposed the advanced approach and built it from the start, from the image pipeline to the multi-chart network. It became the foundation of our final system, which received the top score.

What we did

  • Built an image pipeline in Python (OpenCV, NumPy, SciPy, statsmodels) with adaptive segmentation that follows the printed layers frame by frame.
  • Combined five complementary charts: a non-parametric ECDF band, interface-valley depths, Laplacian texture, SVD/PCA residuals and a Hotelling T² on layer gradients.
  • Controlled the family-wise error rate across all charts with a Šidák correction, setting the trade-off between missed defects and false alarms explicitly.
  • Mapped which charts fire together to isolate the likely physical cause of each alarm.
  • Verified the assumptions behind the limits (Ljung-Box independence tests) and compared against a simpler textbook I-MR baseline.

Why it matters beyond manufacturing

The same logic applies to fraud, transaction and model monitoring: many detectors running in parallel, and a business that tolerates only a set number of false positives.

03 · Accounting, Finance & Control · Politecnico di Milano

What three years of tyre-maker financials say about strategy, and what an AI missed

We benchmarked Pirelli against Michelin and Hankook from their 2022–2024 annual reports, explaining the strategy behind every gap in the numbers. Then we ran the same analysis with a GenAI research tool and audited it against our evidence.

Team of 6Project leadFinancial analysisBenchmarkingGenAI governance

My role

I led the project and carried a large share of the analysis: gathering the data from nine annual reports and audited statements, studying and reclassifying the balance sheets, working out the causes behind each movement, and building the narrative that explains the numbers. I did the same for both benchmarks before pulling everything into the overall comparison, and worked on the prompt design and the final AI audit.

The financial analysis

  • Common-size and horizontal analysis of assets and liabilities, and reclassified balance sheets (net working capital, net financial debt) for all three companies.
  • Profitability (ROE, sustainable growth rate, EBITDA margin, ROCE), liquidity (quick ratio, capex coverage) and working capital (DSO, DPO), with a risk matrix of leverage against return on assets.
  • Each movement traced to a cause in the reports: Pirelli’s “local-for-local” plants raise capital intensity; Hankook’s centralized production drives economies of scale; Michelin trades volume for value.
  • Hankook’s figures converted from won at average annual rates, with ratios used wherever possible to stay currency-neutral.

What we concluded

Pirelli’s focus on high-value tyres pays off in margins, and it has deleveraged steadily, but liquidity is tight: a quick ratio below 1 and payables stretched to 176 days suggest suppliers are financing part of its working capital. Sustaining profitability while easing that pressure is its main challenge as competition in premium tyres intensifies.

The AI audit

The AI reached similar headline conclusions but made two material errors. It called Pirelli’s liquidity “robust” from a current ratio slightly above 1, missing the quick ratio of about 0.7. It also treated Michelin as a pure high-value player, which it is not. Our recommendation: analysts lead data gathering, validation and interpretation; AI supports synthesis and communication. Never outsource understanding.

04 · Operations Management · Politecnico di Milano

A factory that builds one-of-a-kind toolboxes at mass-production speed

Starting from a list of constraints, we found a market gap, designed the product and the online sales process, and sized every machine, buffer and operator of the plant behind it.

Team of 4Push-pull designCapacity planningLeanComputer vision

The idea

Professionals carry expensive tools in generic cases or fragile foam they cut by hand. NEWCO lets customers photograph their tools on an A4 sheet; computer vision extracts the outlines, and the plant mills a custom foam insert and prints the lid. Prices start at €129, €169 and €219 for small, medium and large cases.

My role

I played a central role across the whole project, which spanned the market analysis that identified the gap, the feasibility analysis, machine selection, production-time studies, replenishment at the decoupling point and material sourcing.

Key design choices

  • Target 0.1% of a ~30M-unit global market: 30,000 units a year, 120 a day, a takt time of 3.625 minutes.
  • Placed the customer order decoupling point at a supermarket of 15 standard shells (3 sizes × 5 colors), with reorder points at 3 days of coverage and an ABC rule with a 100-unit minimum batch for slow movers.
  • Upstream injection molding runs make-to-stock in batches; downstream CNC and UV printing run make-to-order, lot size one, with no manual setup.
  • Lean throughout: Jidoka quality gates, digital Kanban, SMED setups and a “water spider” for picking.
  • A 1,100 m² plant near Milan with 12 people, shipping same day for orders placed before 1:00 PM.

05 · L’Oréal Brandstorm 2026 · Strategy & Marketing · Politecnico di Milano

Eternal Memory: a fragrance made from the scent of a cherished object

L’Oréal’s Brandstorm challenge asks students to imagine the next move for one of its brands. Our answer for Maison Margiela was to move luxury fragrance from a shared story to a personal one, and to back the idea with a business model that becomes more profitable as it matures. After pitching to L’Oréal managers, we were selected as Italian national semi-finalists.

Team of 3National semi-finalistGo-to-marketBusiness modelUnit economics

The insight

Personalization in fragrance has hit a ceiling: algorithms help people choose among existing scents, but nobody can wear something that is truly theirs. We built the concept around a deeper need, the fear of a precious memory fading, and turned it into an extension of Maison Margiela’s REPLICA line, whose perfumes evoke shared moments such as a beach walk or a jazz club.

My role

I came up with the concept, worked out how it could be delivered and chose Maison Margiela as the brand to carry it. I managed the relationships with our professors and with the L’Oréal contacts we worked with, designed the customer experience and customer journey, and built the financial feasibility analysis.

How it works

  • In store: the customer brings a personal object, such as a wedding dress or a parent’s scarf, to a Memory Lab, where L’Oréal’s Osmobloom technology captures its scent without damaging it. A Memory Curator refines the profile with the client.
  • At home: an Archive Kit with a porous Memory Stone, a source bottle and glass tools arrives first; the bespoke perfume follows about ten days later, turning the wait into part of the experience.
  • Over time: the formula is stored as a digital twin, so refills are reordered from home through a QR code, with no store visit.

The analysis behind it

We grounded the idea in L’Oréal’s value chain, a PESTEL, SWOT and Porter analysis of luxury fragrance, a business model canvas, a target persona and a product lifecycle plan. Pricing follows premium skimming: a €450 entry experience sits between niche scents (€200–300) and bespoke perfumery (€10,000+), while €180 refills make the scent an everyday ritual rather than a once-only purchase.

06 · Leadership & Innovation · Politecnico di Milano · Field study

How Socomec moved from selling hardware to selling uptime

Socomec, a family-owned French group founded in 1922, makes the backup power systems that keep data centers and operating rooms running. We studied SoLink, its connected service for monitoring those systems remotely, and how leadership guided a multi-generational workforce through the change.

Team of 7Team leadExecutive interviewsChange management30/30 cum laude

My role

I led a team of seven. I managed the relationships with our professors and with Socomec’s managers, conducted the interviews, and took responsibility for the core question of the study: how technological innovation and changes in leadership style reinforced each other.

What we found

  • A new business model. SoLink turned maintenance from “wait for a failure, then repair” into remote, proactive monitoring. Technicians now arrive with a diagnosis and the right spare parts, and the company is moving from one-off hardware sales toward recurring service.
  • A skills shock. Remote diagnostics made some of the senior technicians’ hands-on expertise less central. Trust in data, not the technology itself, was the hardest part.
  • Leadership that changed with the phase. Management first created urgency with clear adoption targets (300, then 700, then 1,000 connected units), then coached and supported technicians, and finally gave a young digital team full autonomy on how to build the solution.
  • Incentives only on top of meaning. Bonuses for connecting devices worked because the purpose of the change had already been explained.

How we analyzed it

We tested the case against innovation frameworks (innovation of meaning vs. solution, product-service systems, technology S-curves, the Kano model) and leadership frameworks (visionary, servant, situational and transformational leadership), using the interviews as evidence rather than illustration.

07 · Strategic Business Decisions · Politecnico di Milano

Growing revenue, shrinking margins: a strategic review of Gerosa Group

Gerosa Group is one of the leading players in flexible packaging, a family-owned, professionally managed group of seven companies across Europe. Its revenue grew by a third between 2019 and 2022, yet its operating margin fell. We analyzed why, and translated the strategy into objectives and measures management could track.

Team of 3Financial analysisStrategy mapBalanced Scorecard

My role

I contributed to every phase of the project, from the industry and financial analysis to the strategy map and the Balanced Scorecard, using AI to help parse the financial and ESG reports.

What we did

  • Mapped the business with Abell’s framework (who, what, how) and assessed the industry with Porter’s five forces: strong rivalry, powerful customers and suppliers, low threat of substitutes.
  • Analyzed 2019–2022 profitability, liquidity and capital structure, and benchmarked ROE, ROI, ROA and ROS against the market median.
  • Ran a SWOT: strengths in portfolio breadth, R&D, in-house engraving and certifications; weaknesses in digitalization; threats from interest-rate and commodity risk.
  • Built a strategy map linking twelve objectives, from staff training and digitalization up to operating profit.
  • Designed a Balanced Scorecard with measures such as on-time-in-full delivery, scrap and complaint volumes, sampling time, R&D investment, training hours, GHG emissions and a digital intensity index.

The key finding

Gerosa works to order, so raw-material price changes reach its selling prices only with a delay. In 2022 costs rose faster than revenue and the margin was squeezed: ROS fell to 1.65% in 2022, against a market median of 4%.
